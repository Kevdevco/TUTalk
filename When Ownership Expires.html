Is it ethical to remove the option to buy and make monthly rent the only way in?

Kevin Shaji Kulathunkal Marketing Ethics & Society 07-11-26

Imagine you buy a hammer. Ten years later the shop calls you. “Keep paying us every month, or the hammer stops working. And so does your access to everything you built with it.” (Pause. Let them react.) You would call that absurd. Yet for the software many of us study with and earn a living with, that is now the standard deal. So my question today is simple. Is it ethical for dominant software companies to take away the option to buy, and make monthly rent the only way in?
02

The marketing angle

Subscription-only is a marketing decision

4 Levers a Marketer Controls
1 · Pricing

Retire the one-time price

Buy-once becomes rent-forever. Adobe’s Creative Cloud Pro moved from US$59.99 to US$69.99 a month in 2025, about US$840 a year (Vigliarolo, 2025).

Is it fair to remove the option to buy?

2 · Retention

Make leaving costly

File formats, learned skill and client expectations tie users in. Cancellation frictions roughly double seller revenue (Einav et al., 2025).

Is lock-in a service, or a trap?

3 · Messaging

Frame the choice

“Pay more or cancel.” Australia’s regulator alleges 2.7 million Microsoft 365 subscribers were not shown a cheaper plan (ACCC, 2025).

Is it honest to hide the cheaper plan?

4 · Exit design

Engineer the cancel flow

76% of 642 subscription sites and apps used at least one dark pattern (FTC, 2024).

Is a harder exit still free choice?

Where does persuasion end and manipulation begin, when the marketer decides what customers can buy, what they see, and how hard it is to leave?

Sources: Vigliarolo (2025); Einav et al. (2025); ACCC (2025); FTC (2024)

Before the evidence, one point about why this is a marketing ethics question. Going subscription-only is not just an engineering or accounting choice. It is a set of marketing decisions. Pricing: the one-time price is retired, and rent replaces it. Retention: file formats and learned skill tie users in, and cancellation friction roughly doubles what sellers earn. Messaging: customers are told to pay more or cancel, and cheaper plans can be kept out of sight. And exit design: the cancel flow is built, not accidental. So the question for this class is where legitimate persuasion ends and manipulation begins.

I want to argue that this is not just a new price tag. It is a transfer of power. The legal scholar Aaron Perzanowski shows how licence terms and software locks let companies keep control of products long after we have paid for them. Consumer researchers hear the same thing from users. Mardon and colleagues interviewed people about their digital possessions, and the title of their study says it all: “Kind of mine, kind of not.” Why does that matter? Because research on psychological ownership finds that the feeling of “mine” grows out of control. In a subscription, control sits with the provider. They decide the price, the features, and whether the door opens tomorrow.

Is lock-in a service, or a trap?

Where everyone relies on one standard, users wait for others to move first, so nobody moves (Biglaiser et al., 2022).

  • Beyond file formatsYears of learned skill and clients who only accept industry-standard files raise the cost of leaving.
  • Why nobody moves firstEach user waits for others to leave, so the incumbent stays dominant (Biglaiser et al., 2022).
  • Who profitsInertia is a revenue source, not a side effect (Einav et al., 2025).

Marketer’s decisionRetention: lock-in is profitable, so building or reducing it is the marketer’s choice.

Sources: Huang (2022); Biglaiser et al. (2022); Einav et al. (2025)

Someone will say: if you don’t like it, switch. But can you? Huang studied proprietary designs in the camera market, and found that being tied to one brand’s lenses created a switching cost about as large as the price of a new camera. Now swap “lens mount” for “file format”. Add years of learned skill, and clients who only accept industry-standard files. Economists show that in markets like this, nobody leaves, because nobody else has left yet. And that inertia is profitable. Using payment-card data, Einav, Klopack and Mahoney estimate that cancellation frictions roughly double what sellers earn from subscribers.

It gets worse, because the exit is often hidden on purpose. In 2024, regulators from 26 countries checked 642 subscription websites and apps. Nearly 76 percent used at least one dark pattern. Researchers call the hard-to-cancel design the “roach motel”: easy to check in, hard to check out. This is not theory. In March 2026, Adobe agreed to a 150 million dollar settlement with the US Department of Justice over hidden early-termination fees and an obstructive cancellation process. Adobe denies wrongdoing. In Australia, the regulator has taken Microsoft to court. It alleges that 2.7 million customers were told to pay up to 45 percent more or cancel, while a cheaper plan was kept out of sight. Lens one · Is the act itself right?

Deontology: duty and autonomy

Kantian Ethics Test

Never treat a person merely as a means. A payment you cannot realistically stop is not consent.

Condition 01 · Choice Huang (2022)
Option A: Buy
Option B: Rent Only

Consent needs a real alternative. Removing the option to buy removes the choice (Huang, 2022).

Condition 02 · Agency Brenncke (2024)
Cognitive Bias Trap → Autonomy Violated

Exploiting biases violates autonomy, whatever the welfare effect (Brenncke, 2024).

Condition 03 · Architecture Helberger et al. (2022)
Asymmetric UI → Universal Risk

Vulnerability is built into digital markets, so anyone can be caught (Helberger et al., 2022).

  • Kant’s ruleNever treat a person merely as a means to your own ends (Rawling, 2023).
  • ConsentA payment you cannot realistically stop is capture, not consent.
  • ResultThe practice fails on duty-based grounds, however much revenue it brings in.

Marketer’s decisionConsent test: if customers fully saw the choice architecture, would they still agree?

Sources: Rawling (2023); Huang (2022); Brenncke (2024); Helberger et al. (2022)

So how should we judge this? First, deontology: the view that some actions are right or wrong in themselves, whatever they earn. Its best-known rule is Kant’s. Never treat a person merely as a means to your own ends. A subscription you freely choose passes that test. But when a dominant company removes the alternative, leans on its own file formats, and designs the exit to fail, your monthly payment is no longer consent. It is capture. Brenncke names this plainly as exploitation that violates autonomy. And Helberger and colleagues argue that this vulnerability is built into digital markets themselves. Anyone can be caught. So on duty-based grounds the practice fails, however much revenue it brings in.

Every benefit survives when a purchase option sits beside the subscription.

  • Inertia revenueAbout half of auto-renewed subscribers kept paying full price, and the extra subscribers did not use the product (Miller et al., 2023).
  • Fine print is no defenceSusceptibility to manipulative design appears across all groups (Zac et al., 2025).
  • Coexistence worksMicrosoft still sells a one-time Office alongside Microsoft 365.

Marketer’s decisionNet welfare: revenue earned from inertia is a harm, not a benefit, when a purchase option could sit beside the subscription.

Sources: Chappell et al. (2024); Lindström et al. (2024); Sharma & Sijariya (2024); Miller et al. (2023); Zac et al. (2025)

Second, utilitarianism, which judges a practice by its total consequences for everyone affected. Let’s be fair. The benefits are real: a lower upfront cost, continuous updates, predictable spending. For a student, a monthly fee beats a huge bill on day one. Now the other column. Payments that never end. Lock-in. And money collected through inertia instead of value. In one large field experiment, about half of auto-renewed subscribers carried on paying full price, and the extra subscribers that auto-renewal produced were not even using the product. Here is the key point. Almost every benefit survives if a purchase option sits beside the subscription. Microsoft still sells a one-time Office. So removing the choice adds harm without adding benefit. The practice fails this test too. If challenged that people should simply read the terms: Zac and colleagues (2025) found susceptibility to manipulative design across all groups.

The bigger picture

The landlord pattern

Political Economy of Software
01 · Ecosystem Tollgate Birch & Cochrane
Platform Gate → Recurring Toll

Rentiership

Big Tech increasingly earns by controlling ecosystems (Birch & Cochrane, 2022).

02 · Digital Landlords Varoufakis
Cloud Capital → Monthly Rent

Cloud rent

Users pay rent to the owners of “cloud capital” (Varoufakis, 2023).

03 · Counterargument Morozov
Feudalism → Capitalism

The objection

“Feudalism” overstates it. This is still capitalism (Morozov, 2022).

Whatever the label, when a tool of work can only be rented, the owner sets the terms.

  • Who is hit hardestFreelancers and students, whose tools of trade can only be rented.
  • Scholars disagree on the labelVaroufakis says “cloud rent”; Morozov says still capitalism. The concern holds either way.
  • TakeawayWhen a tool of work can only be rented, the owner sets the terms.

Marketer’s decisionMarket power: dominant firms set the terms because customers have no comparable alternative.

Sources: Birch & Cochrane (2022); Varoufakis (2023); Morozov (2022)

Step back, and there is a bigger pattern. Birch and Cochrane describe Big Tech moving towards rentiership: earning from controlling an ecosystem, more than from making better things. Yanis Varoufakis goes further. He calls it cloud rent, paid to digital landlords. Not everyone agrees. Evgeny Morozov argues that “feudalism” is an exaggeration, and that this is simply capitalism doing what it does. I accept that. But whatever we call it, when the tools of your trade can only be rented, the landlord sets the terms. For a freelancer or a student, that makes every harm I have listed weigh more.
10

Verdict

Maintenance earns a fee. It does not earn a lifetime lease on someone’s livelihood.

Ongoing Service = Fair Fee ✓ Locked Past Work = Unfair Rent ✕

What fair looks like

Reform 01 · Coexistence Real Choice
Buy Perpetual ✓
Subscribe Cloud ✓

Keep a purchase option beside the subscription.

Why it works: every benefit of subscribing survives, and customers keep a real choice.

Reform 02 · File Sovereignty Past Work
User_Files.psd / .docx Open & Edit ✓

Keep people’s own files editable after they stop paying.

Why it works: paying for service is fair; charging for access to your own past work is not.

Reform 03 · Symmetric UX Clean Exit
1-Click Join = 1-Click Cancel

Make leaving as easy as joining.

Why it works: consent is only real when exit is as easy as entry (Brenncke, 2024).

Marketer’s decisionFor marketers: subscriptions are fine. Removing the buy option, hiding cheaper plans and obstructing exit are the practices to refuse.

Let me be clear about what I am not saying. Subscriptions are not unethical in themselves. Paying for real, ongoing service is fair. What is not fair is removing the door marked “buy”, and then charging rent on a person’s ability to open their own work. So what would fair look like? Three things. One: keep a purchase option beside the subscription. Two: let people open and edit their own files after they stop paying. Three: make leaving as easy as joining. Does delivering updates justify permanent rent? No. Maintenance earns a fee. It does not earn a lifetime lease on someone’s livelihood. (Pause.) When ownership expires, choice expires with it. Thank you.
